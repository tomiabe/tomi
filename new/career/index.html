Career

I have never had a particularly straight career.

I started in technical operations, moved into digital design, spent years working across brand and product, studied creative technology, and gradually found myself taking on problems that did not fit neatly into one discipline.

Today, I work as a Principal Creative Technologist through my independent practice, Tomi Abe Studio. I work with organisations on problems involving strategy, design, technology, research, communication, and the systems connecting them.

The title has changed over time. The curiosity has stayed.

Now

Principal Creative Technologist

Tomi Abe Studio · 2013 to present

Tomi Abe Studio is the thread running through most of my professional life.

I started the practice in 2013 and have kept it running alongside full time, contract, and independent work. Over the years, it has given me the chance to work with startups, growing businesses, institutions, research organisations, and impact driven teams across different markets.

The work has taken many forms. Product and brand strategy. Digital experiences. Research. Data storytelling. Creative technology. Websites and platforms. Design systems. AI and emerging technology.

The common part is usually the problem itself.

Someone has a complicated thing they need to make clearer, more useful, more credible, or more workable. My job is often to understand what is actually going on, figure out what needs to change, and help turn that into something people can use.

Alongside client and studio work, I have spent the last several years teaching, speaking, facilitating workshops, and mentoring designers and digital professionals.

Before this

Building products and digital experiences

For much of the 2020s, my work became increasingly focused on digital products.

I worked with teams building marketplaces, financial products, procurement platforms, talent networks, translation services, fashion platforms, and other digital businesses.

The problems were different, but they often came back to the same things: people struggling to understand what a product does, users getting stuck in important workflows, teams working without a shared design language, or a business finding it difficult to explain its value.

This was when product design became much more than interface design for me.

I became increasingly interested in the decisions underneath the interface.

Why does this work this way?

What does the business need?

What does the user actually need?

What information is missing?

What happens after the screen?

And what needs to be true for the whole thing to keep working?

Working across brand and product

Before that, I spent several years moving between brand, digital design, and product work.

I worked with technology companies, creative agencies, financial organisations, property and market intelligence businesses, communications firms, and early stage ventures.

Some projects were primarily about identity and positioning. Others were websites, campaigns, digital products, or internal systems.

The variety was useful.

It taught me that brand is not only what a company says about itself, and product is not only what happens inside an interface. Both are shaped by the decisions underneath them.

That period also taught me to move between different kinds of people. Founders, engineers, marketers, researchers, executives, designers, and customers do not always describe the same problem in the same way.

A large part of my job became learning how to listen for the problem underneath the brief.

Creative technology

University of the West of England

2018 to 2020

I went back to school because I wanted to explore a different question.

What happens when technology becomes part of the creative material itself?

My postgraduate work in Creative Technology took me into interactive systems, immersive and multisensory technology, accessibility, human computer interaction, and experimental prototyping.

I spent time exploring how immersive technology could support people with autism and how alternative forms of interaction could make technology more accessible.

It was an important shift in my thinking.

Computer science had taught me how systems work. Design had taught me to think about people and experiences. Creative technology gave me room to explore what happens when those two things become less separate.

The early years

From technical operations to design

My first professional role was not in design.

I worked in technical operations and management information at an insurance company in Lagos. I supported users, worked with reporting and data, and saw first hand how much organisations depend on systems that most people only notice when something goes wrong.

That experience stayed with me.

I moved into digital agency work soon after, working across websites, campaigns, brand experiences, and early product work. From there, I began taking on more design responsibility and eventually moved into product design and digital strategy.

I learned by doing a lot of different things.

Some of the early work involved campaign websites, corporate communications, financial systems, developer communities, pension platforms, and digital products. It was not always glamorous work, but it taught me how to work within real constraints and get things shipped.

It also gave me a useful appreciation for the people who have to live with what gets designed.

How do you make something complicated easier for someone to understand and use?

What changed

The biggest change in my career has probably been the kind of problems I am willing to take on.

Early on, I was mostly asked to make things.

A website.
A brand.
An interface.
A campaign.
A product.

Over time, I became more interested in what came before the making.

What is the real problem?

Who is this for?

Why does it matter?

What is the organisation actually trying to achieve?

What information do people need?

What should be designed, and what should be changed somewhere else?

That shift has taken me from being primarily a designer to working more broadly across strategy, technology, research, product, communication, and implementation.

I still care about making good things.

I just care much more now about making the right thing.

Along the way

There are other parts of my career that do not fit neatly into a timeline.

I have taught product design.

I have mentored designers and digital professionals.

I have contributed to open source and volunteered design support for organisations doing social and environmental work.

I have tested emerging creative technologies while they were still taking shape.

I have spoken about AI, design, technology, and creative practice.

I have built independent projects simply because I wanted to understand something better.

Those things matter because they have kept me learning outside the boundaries of client work.

Where I am now

I think of my career less as a progression from one title to another and more as an accumulation of ways of seeing.

Computer science taught me to look at systems.

Design taught me to look at people.

Creative technology taught me to experiment.

Consulting taught me to listen carefully to the problem behind the brief.

Research taught me to question what I think I know.

Building my own practice taught me to take responsibility for the whole thing.

I am still figuring out what comes next.

For now, I am interested in work that sits somewhere between thinking and making, and in problems that are worth spending time on.
